# Enron: Profit on Paper — full narration script

## Act 0 — Cold Open  (0:00)

`0:00` Enron wasn't destroyed because it ran out of customers.  
`0:03` In 2001, its pipelines were still moving gas. Its traders were still making deals.  
`0:09` The real problem had started almost a decade earlier, with a decision about how to count money.  
`0:15` In 1992, Enron got permission to use an accounting method that could turn profits expected in the future... into profits reported today.  
`0:24` The cash didn't have to arrive yet. But on paper, Enron could look spectacular.  
`0:29` And as Wall Street fell in love with the company, keeping that picture alive became critical.  
`0:35` By 2000, Enron was worth nearly seventy billion dollars.  
`0:39` Then, in the fall of 2001, it collapsed in less than two months.  
`0:44` Billions in savings. Thousands of jobs. One of America's most admired companies.  
`0:49` To understand how, you need one accounting idea that changed everything.  

## Act 1 — The Perfect Company  (0:57)

`0:57` Houston, Texas. 1985.  
`0:59` Two natural gas pipeline companies merge into what soon becomes Enron, led by Kenneth Lay.  
`1:05` At first, Enron moves gas. Steady. Predictable. A little boring.  
`1:09` Then Lay brings in a sharp McKinsey consultant named Jeffrey Skilling.  
`1:13` Skilling's big idea was a "Gas Bank." Enron wouldn't just move energy. It would trade it, the way a bank trades money.  
`1:21` It worked. By the late nineties, Enron was trading natural gas, electricity, even internet bandwidth.  
`1:27` Fortune magazine named it "America's Most Innovative Company" six years in a row.  
`1:32` In 2000, Enron reported revenue of about one hundred billion dollars. That August, its stock hit an all-time high. More than ninety dollars a share.  
`1:42` Enron looked like the future.  
`1:44` But underneath that success was a question almost nobody was asking.  
`1:48` How much of that profit had actually arrived as cash?  

## Act 2 — The Accounting Machine  (1:53)

`1:53` To answer that, we have to go back to 1992.  
`1:56` Skilling wanted Enron to use mark-to-market accounting. In January 1992, the Securities and Exchange Commission approved it for Enron's natural gas contracts.  
`2:07` Imagine Enron signs a ten-year contract it expects to earn one hundred million dollars in profit.  
`2:13` Under traditional accounting, that profit shows up gradually, as the gas is delivered and the money comes in.  
`2:19` Under mark-to-market, Enron could estimate what the whole contract was worth today, and book that value as profit now.  
`2:26` Year one. One giant number.  
`2:29` Now, mark-to-market isn't fraud. It's widely used for things with clear market prices.  
`2:34` The danger is in one word. Estimate.  
`2:37` Many of Enron's long-term deals had no market price to check against. Their value rested on Enron's own assumptions.  
`2:44` Make those assumptions a little more optimistic, and the profit gets bigger. Instantly.  
`2:49` And here's the crucial part. The profit is reported now. The cash might arrive years later. Or never match the estimate at all.  
`2:58` Accounting profit is what the financial statements say you earned. Cash flow is the money that actually comes through the door.  
`3:05` At Enron, the two drifted far apart.  
`3:07` One real example: in July 2000, Enron announced a twenty-year deal with Blockbuster for movies on demand.  
`3:14` It never got beyond small pilot tests, and it ended in March 2001.  
`3:19` But Enron had already booked about one hundred and eleven million dollars in profit from it, based largely on projected future earnings.  
`3:28` And once you've reported a contract's future profit today, you can't report it again tomorrow.  
`3:33` So to keep growing, Enron needed more deals. Bigger deals. Every single quarter.  
`3:39` The accounting had built a treadmill. And it was speeding up.  

## Act 3 — Expectations Keep Rising  (3:44)

`3:44` In 1999, Enron's stock rose fifty-six percent. In 2000, another eighty-seven percent.  
`3:51` Stock tickers were installed in the lobbies and elevators. Executives were paid heavily in stock options.  
`3:58` The share price wasn't just a number. It was the scoreboard.  
`4:01` Analysts expected Enron to hit its targets every quarter. And Enron delivered. Again. And again.  
`4:08` But every success raised the bar for the next one.  
`4:10` Meanwhile, some of its biggest bets, like overseas power plants and a broadband network, were struggling.  
`4:16` The machine needed constant growth. And real growth was getting harder to find.  
`4:21` So where do you put the losses, and the debt, when your story can't afford them?  

## Act 4 — Hiding The Problems  (4:27)

`4:27` Enter Andrew Fastow, Enron's chief financial officer from 1998.  
`4:32` His specialty: special-purpose entities.  
`4:34` Basically, a separate company created for one specific job. Companies use them legitimately all the time.  
`4:41` Under the rules back then, an outside investor with as little as three percent of the capital at risk could make the entity count as independent.  
`4:50` And that mattered. Because its assets, and its debt, could stay off Enron's balance sheet.  
`4:55` Enron used them again and again. Chewco. LJM. And the Raptors, named after the dinosaurs in Jurassic Park.  
`5:02` But in several cases, the outside money wasn't really outside.  
`5:06` Chewco, it later emerged, didn't meet the three percent rule at all.  
`5:10` The Raptors made Enron's investment losses look hedged, covered by someone else. But that someone else was effectively backed by Enron's own stock.  
`5:19` A later investigation for Enron's board found these deals inflated reported earnings by almost one billion dollars, in just over a year.  
`5:29` Then there was the conflict of interest.  
`5:31` Fastow didn't just design the LJM partnerships. He ran them, while serving as C.F.O. Enron's board approved it, as an exception to its own code of conduct.  
`5:41` The investigation found Fastow was involved on both sides of these deals, and made more than thirty million dollars from them.  
`5:49` On paper, Enron looked stronger than ever.  
`5:52` In reality, risk was piling up in places investors could barely see.  

## Act 5 — The Warning Signs  (5:58)

`5:58` But some people started looking.  
`6:00` March 2001. In Fortune, the same magazine that kept calling Enron innovative, reporter Bethany McLean asked: "Is Enron Overpriced?"  
`6:10` The real question: how exactly does Enron make its money?  
`6:14` The financial statements were nearly impossible to decode. The debt was growing. And the cash didn't seem to line up with the profits.  
`6:21` In August, just six months after becoming C.E.O., Jeffrey Skilling resigned, citing personal reasons.  
`6:28` Kenneth Lay took the job back, and assured the market: no change in performance or outlook.  
`6:33` The next day, a vice president named Sherron Watkins sent Lay an anonymous letter.  
`6:38` One line would become famous.  
`6:40` "I am incredibly nervous that we will implode in a wave of accounting scandals."  
`6:46` By the end of August, the stock was below thirty-five dollars.  
`6:50` The cracks were no longer invisible.  

## Act 6 — The Collapse  (6:54)

`6:54` October sixteenth, 2001.  
`6:56` Enron reports a third-quarter loss of six hundred and eighteen million dollars, after one point zero one billion in charges.  
`7:04` It also reveals a one point two billion dollar cut to shareholders' equity, tied to partnerships run by its own C.F.O.  
`7:12` October twenty-second. The S.E.C. starts asking questions. The stock falls to around twenty dollars.  
`7:18` October twenty-fourth. Fastow is out.  
`7:20` November eighth. Enron restates its results all the way back to 1997. Hundreds of millions in reported profit, erased. Hundreds of millions in debt, suddenly back on the books.  
`7:32` And a trading company runs on trust. Partners have to believe you'll pay.  
`7:37` That trust was vanishing.  
`7:38` November ninth. A lifeline. Dynegy, a Houston rival, agrees to buy Enron.  
`7:43` But the more Dynegy looked, the worse it got.  
`7:46` November twenty-eighth. Credit agencies cut Enron to junk. Dynegy walks away.  
`7:51` That day, Enron's stock closes at sixty-one cents.  
`7:54` December second, 2001. Enron files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection.  
`8:00` With sixty-three point four billion dollars in assets, it was the largest bankruptcy in American history at the time.  
`8:07` From more than ninety dollars a share, to under one dollar. In about fifteen months.  

## Act 7 — Human Consequences  (8:15)

`8:15` But Enron was never just a stock chart.  
`8:18` Within days of the filing, about four thousand employees in Houston were laid off.  
`8:23` Many had built their retirement on Enron stock. It made up roughly sixty percent of the company's 401(k) plan.  
`8:30` Employees lost more than a billion dollars in retirement savings.  
`8:35` Investors lost tens of billions of dollars in market value.  
`8:39` And Enron's auditor, Arthur Andersen, one of the five biggest accounting firms in the world, went down with it.  
`8:45` Its staff had shredded Enron documents. In June 2002, the firm was convicted of obstruction of justice, and its business collapsed.  
`8:54` The Supreme Court later overturned the conviction. By then, almost nothing was left.  
`9:00` Fastow pleaded guilty. Skilling was convicted and sentenced to twenty-four years, later reduced. Lay was convicted too, but died before sentencing.  

## Act 8 — The Final Reveal  (9:12)

`9:12` So what really destroyed Enron?  
`9:15` Not one accounting method. Not one partnership. Not one executive.  
`9:19` It was a combination. Aggressive estimates that pulled future profits into the present. Debt and losses moved where investors could barely see them. A C.F.O. on both sides of the deal. Disclosures almost no one could understand. And relentless pressure to keep the numbers climbing.  
`9:36` Mark-to-market didn't destroy Enron on its own. But it helped open the gap at the center of this story.  
`9:42` The gap between looking profitable, and actually generating cash.  
`9:47` Enron spent years showing Wall Street what it wanted to see.  
`9:51` But accounting could only delay the moment when expectations collided with reality.  
`9:56` And once investors stopped believing the numbers, Enron didn't have years left.  
`10:01` It had weeks.
