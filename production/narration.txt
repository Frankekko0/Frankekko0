## ACT0 COLD OPEN
Enron wasn't destroyed because it ran out of customers.
In 2001, its pipelines were still moving gas. Its traders were still making deals.
The real problem had started almost a decade earlier, with a decision about how to count money.
In 1992, Enron got permission to use an accounting method that could turn profits expected in the future... into profits reported today.
The cash didn't have to arrive yet. But on paper, Enron could look spectacular.
And as Wall Street fell in love with the company, keeping that picture alive became critical.
By 2000, Enron was worth nearly seventy billion dollars.
Then, in the fall of 2001, it collapsed in less than two months.
Billions in savings. Thousands of jobs. One of America's most admired companies.
To understand how, you need one accounting idea that changed everything.

## ACT1 THE PERFECT COMPANY
Houston, Texas. 1985.
Two natural gas pipeline companies merge into what soon becomes Enron, led by Kenneth Lay.
At first, Enron moves gas. Steady. Predictable. A little boring.
Then Lay brings in a sharp McKinsey consultant named Jeffrey Skilling.
Skilling's big idea was a "Gas Bank." Enron wouldn't just move energy. It would trade it, the way a bank trades money.
It worked. By the late nineties, Enron was trading natural gas, electricity, even internet bandwidth.
Fortune magazine named it "America's Most Innovative Company" six years in a row.
In 2000, Enron reported revenue of about one hundred billion dollars. That August, its stock hit an all-time high. More than ninety dollars a share.
Enron looked like the future.
But underneath that success was a question almost nobody was asking.
How much of that profit had actually arrived as cash?

## ACT2 THE ACCOUNTING MACHINE
To answer that, we have to go back to 1992.
Skilling wanted Enron to use mark-to-market accounting. In January 1992, the Securities and Exchange Commission approved it for Enron's natural gas contracts.
Imagine Enron signs a ten-year contract it expects to earn one hundred million dollars in profit.
Under traditional accounting, that profit shows up gradually, as the gas is delivered and the money comes in.
Under mark-to-market, Enron could estimate what the whole contract was worth today, and book that value as profit now.
Year one. One giant number.
Now, mark-to-market isn't fraud. It's widely used for things with clear market prices.
The danger is in one word. Estimate.
Many of Enron's long-term deals had no market price to check against. Their value rested on Enron's own assumptions.
Make those assumptions a little more optimistic, and the profit gets bigger. Instantly.
And here's the crucial part. The profit is reported now. The cash might arrive years later. Or never match the estimate at all.
Accounting profit is what the financial statements say you earned. Cash flow is the money that actually comes through the door.
At Enron, the two drifted far apart.
One real example: in July 2000, Enron announced a twenty-year deal with Blockbuster for movies on demand.
It never got beyond small pilot tests, and it ended in March 2001.
But Enron had already booked about one hundred and eleven million dollars in profit from it, based largely on projected future earnings.
And once you've reported a contract's future profit today, you can't report it again tomorrow.
So to keep growing, Enron needed more deals. Bigger deals. Every single quarter.
The accounting had built a treadmill. And it was speeding up.

## ACT3 EXPECTATIONS KEEP RISING
In 1999, Enron's stock rose fifty-six percent. In 2000, another eighty-seven percent.
Stock tickers were installed in the lobbies and elevators. Executives were paid heavily in stock options.
The share price wasn't just a number. It was the scoreboard.
Analysts expected Enron to hit its targets every quarter. And Enron delivered. Again. And again.
But every success raised the bar for the next one.
Meanwhile, some of its biggest bets, like overseas power plants and a broadband network, were struggling.
The machine needed constant growth. And real growth was getting harder to find.
So where do you put the losses, and the debt, when your story can't afford them?

## ACT4 HIDING THE PROBLEMS
Enter Andrew Fastow, Enron's chief financial officer from 1998.
His specialty: special-purpose entities.
Basically, a separate company created for one specific job. Companies use them legitimately all the time.
Under the rules back then, an outside investor with as little as three percent of the capital at risk could make the entity count as independent.
And that mattered. Because its assets, and its debt, could stay off Enron's balance sheet.
Enron used them again and again. Chewco. LJM. And the Raptors, named after the dinosaurs in Jurassic Park.
But in several cases, the outside money wasn't really outside.
Chewco, it later emerged, didn't meet the three percent rule at all.
The Raptors made Enron's investment losses look hedged, covered by someone else. But that someone else was effectively backed by Enron's own stock.
A later investigation for Enron's board found these deals inflated reported earnings by almost one billion dollars, in just over a year.
Then there was the conflict of interest.
Fastow didn't just design the LJM partnerships. He ran them, while serving as C.F.O. Enron's board approved it, as an exception to its own code of conduct.
The investigation found Fastow was involved on both sides of these deals, and made more than thirty million dollars from them.
On paper, Enron looked stronger than ever.
In reality, risk was piling up in places investors could barely see.

## ACT5 THE WARNING SIGNS
But some people started looking.
March 2001. In Fortune, the same magazine that kept calling Enron innovative, reporter Bethany McLean asked: "Is Enron Overpriced?"
The real question: how exactly does Enron make its money?
The financial statements were nearly impossible to decode. The debt was growing. And the cash didn't seem to line up with the profits.
In August, just six months after becoming C.E.O., Jeffrey Skilling resigned, citing personal reasons.
Kenneth Lay took the job back, and assured the market: no change in performance or outlook.
The next day, a vice president named Sherron Watkins sent Lay an anonymous letter.
One line would become famous.
"I am incredibly nervous that we will implode in a wave of accounting scandals."
By the end of August, the stock was below thirty-five dollars.
The cracks were no longer invisible.

## ACT6 THE COLLAPSE
October sixteenth, 2001.
Enron reports a third-quarter loss of six hundred and eighteen million dollars, after one point zero one billion in charges.
It also reveals a one point two billion dollar cut to shareholders' equity, tied to partnerships run by its own C.F.O.
October twenty-second. The S.E.C. starts asking questions. The stock falls to around twenty dollars.
October twenty-fourth. Fastow is out.
November eighth. Enron restates its results all the way back to 1997. Hundreds of millions in reported profit, erased. Hundreds of millions in debt, suddenly back on the books.
And a trading company runs on trust. Partners have to believe you'll pay.
That trust was vanishing.
November ninth. A lifeline. Dynegy, a Houston rival, agrees to buy Enron.
But the more Dynegy looked, the worse it got.
November twenty-eighth. Credit agencies cut Enron to junk. Dynegy walks away.
That day, Enron's stock closes at sixty-one cents.
December second, 2001. Enron files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection.
With sixty-three point four billion dollars in assets, it was the largest bankruptcy in American history at the time.
From more than ninety dollars a share, to under one dollar. In about fifteen months.

## ACT7 HUMAN CONSEQUENCES
But Enron was never just a stock chart.
Within days of the filing, about four thousand employees in Houston were laid off.
Many had built their retirement on Enron stock. It made up roughly sixty percent of the company's 401(k) plan.
Employees lost more than a billion dollars in retirement savings.
Investors lost tens of billions of dollars in market value.
And Enron's auditor, Arthur Andersen, one of the five biggest accounting firms in the world, went down with it.
Its staff had shredded Enron documents. In June 2002, the firm was convicted of obstruction of justice, and its business collapsed.
The Supreme Court later overturned the conviction. By then, almost nothing was left.
Fastow pleaded guilty. Skilling was convicted and sentenced to twenty-four years, later reduced. Lay was convicted too, but died before sentencing.

## ACT8 THE FINAL REVEAL
So what really destroyed Enron?
Not one accounting method. Not one partnership. Not one executive.
It was a combination. Aggressive estimates that pulled future profits into the present. Debt and losses moved where investors could barely see them. A C.F.O. on both sides of the deal. Disclosures almost no one could understand. And relentless pressure to keep the numbers climbing.
Mark-to-market didn't destroy Enron on its own. But it helped open the gap at the center of this story.
The gap between looking profitable, and actually generating cash.
Enron spent years showing Wall Street what it wanted to see.
But accounting could only delay the moment when expectations collided with reality.
And once investors stopped believing the numbers, Enron didn't have years left.
It had weeks.
